> This is not simply a book about Japan. It is a mirror: another society's thirty-year struggle after a bubble burst, reflecting recurring economic cycles, policy dilemmas, and the wounds carried by generations.

---

## Part One: Decisions in a Wave of Unemployment

### Chapter One: Protecting Jobs—A Seemingly Right “Mistake”

In 1989, the Nikkei reached an all-time high and began to fall. By 1991, it had broken below key levels. Yet almost everyone believed this was only a brief correction. At the end of 1990, Japan's media kept returning to one phrase: **“unrealized gains.”** Everyone in the market was convinced that the profits they had lost would eventually return.

They were wrong.

After the bubble burst, Japanese companies faced a dilemma with almost no way out. Collapsing profits coincided with excess capacity; market logic called for bankruptcies, liquidation, and the removal of that capacity. But Japan's deeply rooted tradition of lifetime employment and its corporate culture of cross-shareholding made large-scale layoffs and business failures socially unacceptable. Faced with a choice between “protecting jobs” and “protecting development through market adjustment,” the government yielded to social pressure and made employment stability the overriding priority.

This dilemma has played out repeatedly across economies: social stability requires preserving jobs, while economic vitality requires shutting down unproductive capacity. Both cannot always be secured at once. Japan's lesson is that when government forcibly holds back the market, the short-term figures can indeed look reassuring—unemployment stayed below 5%—but the price can be paid in installments over the next thirty years.

**In corporate restructuring**, the government encouraged “the large absorbing the small” and mergers among groups of companies. Large corporations or banks took over loss-making smaller businesses to preserve jobs and absorb excess capacity. On the surface, this solved the problem. In practice, it merely shifted the burden from one set of shoulders to another.

**In employment reform**, the government introduced a two-track labor market. Existing permanent employees retained lifetime employment under the old rules, while legislation such as the Worker Dispatching Act strongly encouraged companies to hire new workers on fixed-term contracts or through staffing agencies. This reduced companies' immediate labor costs at the expense of training and long-term employment security for new university graduates.

**The costs were severe:**

- **The proliferation of zombie companies.** Inefficient firms kept alive by bank financing continued to occupy capital and labor that should have flowed toward innovation. This legacy remains today.
- **The collapse of the financial system.** Banks accumulated enormous bad debts while rescuing companies. Expectations of a government backstop allowed risk to spread until a wave of bank failures erupted in the mid-to-late 1990s. All ten of the largest banks were restructured; none escaped.
- **A missed opportunity for industrial upgrading.** Scarce capital went toward sustaining old capacity and rescuing old companies rather than researching new industries. Japan consequently missed the semiconductor and internet revolutions of the 1990s. Its technological position shifted from leader to follower—an entire industrial trajectory bent by administrative intervention.
- **The sacrifice of a generation: the “employment ice age” cohort.** To contain costs, companies sharply reduced graduate recruitment and training. Students graduating between 1993 and 2003 faced falling employment rates, stagnant starting salaries, and blocked career paths. Many ended up in unstable, poorly paid non-regular work. Decades later, their incomes remained the lowest among generations. Short-term social calm was purchased with an entire generation's careers.

**The jusen crisis** was a microcosm of property finance. The government's handling of specialist housing loan companies, known as jusen, became a textbook example of how not to respond to a crisis. Extending its policy of protecting employment, the government repeatedly required banks to cover the jusen's huge bad debts to prevent a chain of property-company failures and job losses: delay a little longer, offer a little more protection. Risk eventually spread throughout the financial system, and the jusen became a direct trigger for the wave of bank failures.

> **The central lesson:** Policies that buy short-term social stability at the expense of long-term development and young people's futures will eventually leave a deficit in history's accounts.

### Chapter Two: Unemployment and a Forgotten Generation

They were the nearly twenty million people born during Japan's second postwar baby boom in the 1970s. During their first twenty years, they watched Japanese companies sweep across global markets. They saw prestigious employers scramble for recruits at the height of the bubble and heard stories of entrepreneurs raising a billion yen at a first meeting. Their imagined futures were expansive: graduate from an elite university, join a major company, earn ten million yen a year.

Then the bubble burst.

They collided head-on with Japan's lost thirty years. An unending downturn immersed the rest of their lives in the pain of low pay and unemployment.

**Overstaffing began with society-wide economic optimism.** The economic miracle of the 1980s persuaded businesses that Japanese industry would eventually conquer the world. Cheap financing in a low-interest-rate environment provided the ideal fuel for this collective euphoria.

**Delaying entry into the labor market proved futile.** Faced with a bleak graduate job market, the government pursued two measures: directing graduates toward rural areas and expanding postgraduate enrollment. The idea was strikingly simple—if students entered the job market later, the pressure would ease for a while. From 1996, however, the government gradually stopped investing in large infrastructure projects. The rural jobs created by that spending quickly disappeared, forcing graduates back into the major cities. The additional supply of graduates intensified the shock. Laying off older employees would have turned their mortgages and personal loans into a financial time bomb. Once again, the choice was to sacrifice the young.

By the fifth year after the bubble burst, many university students had realized that no amount of effort would deliver the lives they had planned. A culture of retreating into the home began to grow. Cultural consumption and emotional consolation offered substitutes for economic opportunity in the non-material world.

From 1997, Japan's policymakers were overwhelmed by the crisis. Four prime ministers came and went in five years, and the crisis was barely brought under control through an additional ¥40 trillion in government bond issuance. By then, attention to graduates' circumstances had disappeared at every level of society: there were simply too many companies to save. The bitterest fact was that, over those five years, the government did not pass a single law specifically addressing graduate employment. This cohort became known as **“the forgotten generation.”**

### Chapter Three: Stay in the City or Go Home? The Rise and Fall of the Civil-Service Rush

After the bubble burst, Japan saw a nationwide rush to take civil-service examinations. Government work was once called “a job fit for the gods,” the ultimate shelter from a weak economy. That enthusiasm lasted only about ten years.

**The dilemma of rural revitalization.** Another reason for the policy was to counter the overheating of major cities during the bubble and the severe imbalance in regional resource allocation. Between 1986 and 1990, the three largest metropolitan areas drew in an astonishing ¥1,000 trillion in capital, while all the other smaller cities combined attracted less than ¥200 trillion.

Rural revitalization came in two waves. The first was **large-scale rural infrastructure construction, from 1992 to 1995**. Massive public investment created many construction jobs in the short term and drew workers back from the cities. The second was **rural tourism development, from 1995 onward**. As the effects of infrastructure spending faded, investment was encouraged in rural resorts and tourism projects. For a time, rural household incomes overtook urban incomes.

Yet creating jobs through sustained fiscal spending and credit expansion meant borrowing against the future. When the Asian financial crisis struck in 1997, government and corporate finances came under pressure simultaneously. Rural investment dried up, and many tourism projects were abandoned unfinished. The eventual response was the **Great Heisei Municipal Mergers**: to cut expenditure, the government promoted municipal consolidation nationwide. Nearly half of local grassroots positions disappeared, destroying the rural labor market that had been artificially created.

**The emergence of a drifting generation.** As rural jobs vanished, large numbers of young people were forced back into the major cities. Those cities were now experiencing a wave of financial failures and a deeply depressed job market. These young people were trapped between hometowns they could not return to and cities where they could not establish a life. Most could find only unstable, low-paid agency work. They were forced to give up marriage, homeownership, and children. They became a distinct impoverished generation, with lasting damage to their earnings and careers and almost no prospect of recovery as they aged.

A less visible shift was the use of certain industries as reservoirs for surplus labor. To absorb unemployed workers returning to cities, the government relaxed entry requirements in sectors such as taxis. The result was a surge in workers and a collapse in earnings. Tokyo taxi drivers' pay fell from 0.9 times the average wage to 0.54 times. Unemployment had been converted into low-income, low-quality employment. Its appearance changed; the underlying problem did not.

## Part Two: A Generation Unable to Make Peace with Itself

### Chapter Four: Beginning Life in a Lost Economy

**“Four hours and you pass; five hours and you fail” was once a familiar saying across Japan.** Sleep four hours a night, and you would get into your chosen university; sleep five, and your name would be missing from the admission list. In the 1970s and 1980s, university admission rates were below 40%, while society was saturated with stories of ten-million-yen salaries at major companies. Scarce educational opportunities, fierce competition among peers, and high rewards from employment formed an intensely pressurized furnace.

The tutoring industry entered an extraordinary boom in the 1970s. The number of cram schools nationwide exceeded 10,000 in 1976, doubled to 20,000 five years later, and quadrupled over a decade. In Tokyo, the center of the tutoring industry, residential cram-school camps appeared. Primary-school children wore headbands declaring that hard work would bring victory, slept fewer than six hours a night, and studied intensively for more than a month at a time.

The sociologist Ezra Vogel described the tension of Japan's examination season. Children became the absolute center of family life. Mothers silently placed meals on their desks, afraid that any sound might interrupt their concentration. Fathers received permission from their bosses to return home early to help with schoolwork. Across society, from convenience-store conversations to office breaks, discussion revolved around children's exams.

On the day university results were posted, only parents came to check them. An unwritten rule had emerged: parents should read the lists on their children's behalf, for fear that a child who learned they had failed might take their own life on the way home.

From the 1970s, examination competition spread from primary school through high school. Japan's media called it **juken jigoku—“examination hell.”** These children spent their childhoods and adolescence driven to extremes by anxiety, pressure, and the belief that effort would inevitably lead to success.

**Then the bubble burst.** In the 1990s, those born in the 1970s finally made it through university, only to encounter the employment ice age. They became trapped in a self-defeating loop. Society told them that hard work brought success, but years of effort had produced no positive response at all. They had good degrees and could not find good jobs. The distance between aspiration and reality left them unable either to win society's understanding or to make peace with themselves.

From 1997, suicide rates among young Japanese people began to climb, nearly tripling by 2003 and remaining elevated for years afterward. It was common to encounter thirty-year-olds with no job, no money for rent, and nowhere to live. Pride kept them from asking their parents for help, and they rarely applied for government assistance. They blamed themselves for every failure and never found a way to reconcile themselves to it.

> Yet no one told them: the failure was not theirs. Society was ill.

**Middle age, and contempt from their own children.** In Japan, an “agency-worker household” carried an insulting connotation: both parents were temporary agency workers, with unstable jobs and no savings. Their average annual earnings were ¥2.63 million, against a relative poverty threshold of ¥2 million. Most struggled close to that line. A 2020 survey found that more than half of parents in these households had cut back to two meals a day, while about 40% of their children could not count on even two meals a day during school holidays.

**Approaching old age without direction.** The employment ice age generation has passed through childhood, youth, and middle age and is now moving inexorably toward old age. The number of older Japanese people living alone has approached six million, about half with annual incomes below the minimum subsistence assistance standard. Retirement provision is becoming the next time bomb.

### Chapter Five: Credential Depreciation—When a Degree Stops Paying

They were the last generation shaped by Japan's elite-education promise. Raised to believe that qualifications were everything, they experienced the most intense parental pressure to achieve. They wagered their entire lives on education, then watched the degrees they had spent their early lives pursuing lose value visibly after the bubble burst.

**Two forces drove credential depreciation:**

- **Supply:** Baby-boom cohorts graduated in large numbers during the 1990s. The total number of graduates surged by 40% from the 1980s.
- **Demand:** The economic bubble burst, and corporate recruitment contracted sharply.

The largest graduating classes met the smallest number of vacancies. This is the basic formula for credential depreciation, and it works in any economy.

**The process unfolded in four stages, like a disaster in slow motion:**

| Stage | Period | Central development |
|------|------|----------|
| **Stage One** | Early 1990s | Campus recruitment collapsed: the number of participating companies fell 70% from the bubble years. Graduates were forced into the general job market without the advantages reserved for new graduates. Starting salaries stagnated or declined. The mechanism that gave a degree its initial price was the first to break down. |
| **Stage Two** | Mid-to-late 1990s | Disorderly hiring and competition through family connections: the government abolished the campus recruitment system and promoted agency work. Students began pursuing internships and certifications in their first year, entering an escalating credentials race. The best jobs went to well-connected children, severely eroding education's promise of a fair competition. By 1999, the wage gap between university graduates and junior-high-school graduates had become negligible. |
| **Stage Three** | Early 2000s | Liberalization of agency employment: 2.8 million unemployed people gathered in cities. The government fully lifted restrictions on agency work, and staffing intermediaries established a hierarchy of labor grades. Degrees became detached from pay; university and high-school graduates were often placed in the same salary grade. |
| **Stage Four** | After 2005 | The marketization of education and an investment paradox: university corporatization drove tuition fees sharply higher. A student at a national university faced lifetime expenditure of about ¥50 million, yet low-paid agency work awaited after graduation. “High investment, no return” transformed social attitudes. The share of high-school students taking university entrance examinations fell below 50%, and Japan entered a society with lower levels of educational attainment. |

**Expanding master's and doctoral enrollment: buying more as the value falls.** In the early 1990s, Japan remained absorbed in the vision of building national strength through technology. The government introduced a strategy of building the country through science and launched a plan to double graduate-school enrollment, aiming to add 100,000 master's students over ten years. Implementation quickly ran out of control. The target was exceeded within five years, and at the peak the excess reached twice the planned amount. Private capital poured into education. Graduate schools at private universities proliferated, while teaching staff and facilities failed to keep pace. Student-to-faculty ratios deteriorated from 2.7 to more than 5, and graduate schools became places to pass the time, with supervisors unable to provide adequate guidance.

When the government finally recognized the problem, it was afraid to stop the expansion. Doing so would send vast numbers of applicants into the labor market. It fell into the familiar pattern of using a larger mistake to cover up the previous one. The response distorted both ends of the system: tighter assessment of master's students sent delayed graduations soaring, while further expansion at doctoral level, including a plan for 10,000 postdoctoral researchers, pushed the crisis further into the future.

Ultimately, the science-based national strategy underpinning the expansion failed, symbolized by South Korea overtaking Japan in semiconductors. Fiscal austerity contracted academia, while prolonged stagnation contracted industry. Large numbers of master's and doctoral graduates became poorly paid temporary teachers, generally earning less than ¥2 million a year, or agency workers. The highly educated working poor became a defining social phenomenon of the period.

**The cycle in the value of a degree: evidence from the United States.** A society organized around educational credentials needs rapid economic growth to take root. The United States from the end of World War II through the 1960s exemplified this. The first collapse of that promise came with the oil crisis and stagflation of the 1970s. Baby-boom graduates encountered economic stagnation, the earnings advantage of a university degree narrowed sharply, and qualifications lost much of their value. Hippie culture and the Beat Generation arose from this disillusionment with the equation of education and success. After Reagan-era economic policies drove a recovery in the 1980s, degrees were revalued and university enrollment recovered. Following the 2008 financial crisis, another round of credential depreciation began from almost the same starting point. The pendulum keeps swinging: economic expansion and contraction leave their marks directly on the price of a diploma.

## Part Three: Working Lives

### Chapter Seven: The Rise and Fall of the Civil-Service Rush—From “A Job Fit for the Gods” to Few Takers

**The bubble years: the 1980s.** In a society chasing rapid wealth, stable but dull government jobs with fixed salaries held little appeal. Graduates of elite universities who entered the civil service were mocked as life's losers. Local government offices were jokingly called “a paradise for high-school graduates.” The decisive factor was the public-private pay gap. Annual bonuses in private finance, property, and major trading houses could reach ¥4–6 million. Middle managers could receive ¥10 million or even more than ¥100 million. Local civil servants, by comparison, earned only about ¥5 million a year in total.

**The rush: the 1990s through the early 2000s.** After the bubble burst, finance and property collapsed. The promise that civil servants would never lose their jobs became scarce and valuable almost overnight. In 1998, 64% of graduating university students took civil-service examinations. The government launched large infrastructure programs to stimulate the economy. Strong local revenues allowed public-sector pay to soar: municipal bus drivers in Yokohama earned close to ¥9 million a year. Civil-service work became **“a job fit for the gods.”**

**The decline: after 1998.** This golden age rested on the shifting sands of fiscal stimulus. After the Asian financial crisis, public finances approached collapse. Large infrastructure investments were withdrawn, and local government revenues plunged. What followed was a steep rise in workloads, with nearly 30% working more than 80 hours of overtime a month; five consecutive years of pay cuts, exceeding 40% cumulatively; and continuing reductions in staffing. By 2023, the average annual income of local civil servants had fallen back to ¥4.7 million, only half its peak.

**The position in the 2020s.** Applicant numbers have continued to decline, with roughly three candidates for each place. Between 2012 and 2022, the number of University of Tokyo graduates entering central government fell by 60%. IT and finance once again moved far ahead on pay, with comparable jobs offering as much as twice a civil servant's salary. The security of an “iron rice bowl” was outweighed by low pay, intense pressure, and poor promotion prospects. A new generation faced an entirely different labor market and voted with its feet.

### Chapter Eight: The Fall of Teachers—From Respected Professionals to an Exploitative Occupation

Over roughly thirty years, Japan's teachers fell from the rank of respected professionals addressed as sensei, alongside lawyers and doctors, into an occupation notorious for death from overwork. It began with a serious misreading of demographic trends.

Scarred by the pressures of the first two baby booms, Japanese society came to believe that a baby boom was inevitable every twenty to thirty years. In the 1980s, the government expanded teacher recruitment nationwide in preparation for an expected third wave. By 1991, teacher numbers reached an all-time high of 1.05 million. Then the baby boom failed to arrive. Births in the 1990s were five million below expectations, the bubble burst, and public finances deteriorated rapidly. The large, highly paid teaching workforce assembled for the anticipated boom went from strategic reserve to heavy burden almost overnight.

**Three rounds of reform placed teachers under systematic pressure:**

- **First:** New teacher recruitment was sharply reduced, by nearly 60% by 1995. More than one million qualified applicants could not enter the profession.
- **Second:** Temporary contract teachers became widespread, reaching one-sixth of the workforce while receiving only half the pay of permanent teachers. Teachers were also assigned substantial administrative duties, and a system requiring them to teach across subjects was introduced. Working hours exceeded 62 a week, crossing the statutory threshold associated with death from overwork. Teaching began to be described as an exploitative occupation.
- **Third:** Teachers' employment status was separated from that of national civil servants, and local governments became responsible for their pay. Local authorities immediately began cutting salaries, with an overall reduction approaching 30%. Average teacher pay at one point fell below that of agency workers. Strict performance assessments were introduced, with dismissal a consequence of failing them.

**The lowest point:** In 2010, about 30% of teachers suffered from mild depression, and 1% resigned because of stress that year. Teaching became one of Japan's occupations with the highest risks of suicide and mental illness. Economic decline fractured families, encouraged disengagement from school, and spread school violence. Society reduced these problems to “incompetent teachers.” Teachers became scapegoats for a society's accumulated frustration.

### Chapter Nine: Healthcare's Dark Decade—A Systemic Breakdown

Between 1992 and 2002, Japan's healthcare system endured a perfect storm.

**Population aging delivered the first shock.** Japan was East Asia's first deeply aged society. Older patients consumed about four times the medical resources of younger people. The disease burden shifted from acute infections toward chronic illnesses requiring long-term management, and demand grew exponentially. The government had badly underestimated this trend. In the late 1980s, it even concluded that there were too many doctors and began cutting their numbers, leaving a severe shortage of reserves when the crisis arrived.

**The breakdown unfolded in four steps:**

1. **Doctor shortages and punishing overwork.** By 1994, the shortfall had reached 94,000 doctors. Four in ten worked more than 80 hours a week, at the threshold associated with death from overwork.
2. **Cost-control reform and deteriorating quality.** The government tied cost containment to doctors' assessments and promotions. Doctors were forced to prioritize cheaper drugs and treatments. Medical accidents surged, with 60% directly connected to excessive cost cutting. About 11,000 doctors resigned from public hospitals because of ethical distress or institutional pressure.
3. **The collapse of trust between doctors and patients.** Encouraged by media coverage, patients directed their anger over declining care toward frontline doctors. After the government introduced regulations penalizing negligent medical treatment, the police intervened forcefully. An era of harsher medical punishment followed: in just a few years, the number of doctors sentenced nearly equaled the total of the previous forty years. This produced a widespread “cesarean-section effect,” in which doctors chose the most cautious treatment to avoid risk rather than the one most beneficial to the patient.
4. **Violence and an exodus of doctors.** Social hostility reached a peak, and terror attacks against doctors emerged. In 2003, nearly 3,000 clinical doctors left their posts.

**The “slavery” of resident doctors.** Residents were not recognized as employees, allowing hospitals to use them as unpaid, cheap labor. A 1998 case involving a resident's death from overwork at a Kansai hospital became a turning point. The victim's father was a labor-law expert. After prolonged litigation, a court ruled for the first time that an employment relationship existed between a resident and the hospital. The case helped bring about a fundamental reform of the residency system in 2004 and became a starting point for rebuilding healthcare.

**Healthcare renewal** centered on two approaches: redirecting demand and improving efficiency. Long-term care insurance moved rehabilitation and everyday care for older people out of acute hospitals and into community and home-care services. A single institutional change thus redirected large volumes of “social hospitalization”—hospital stays driven by care needs rather than acute illness—to local services. Estimates put the labor savings at the equivalent of tens of thousands of doctors. The DPC payment system then changed the logic of reimbursement. Previously, hospitals billed for each service provided. Under the new approach, a diagnosis attracted a total payment, early discharge was rewarded, and the hospital bore the cost of an extended stay. The rules shifted the relationship between hospitals and patients from a shared financial interest in longer stays toward a shared interest in efficiency.

The costs were also visible. Seeking higher patient turnover, hospitals frequently discharged people too early, increasing postoperative readmissions and complications. Faced with healthcare's impossible triangle—quality, efficiency, and affordability—Japan gave up some of the highest attainable quality to secure universal access and control costs.

> Japan's healthcare recovery is a textbook account of the difficult search for a balance between efficiency and fairness, using intricate and unsentimental institutional design under extreme constraints.

### Chapter Ten: The Disappointment of Science and Engineering—Why Were Engineers Discouraged from Their Field?

One counterintuitive historical lesson is that **choosing science or engineering in a downturn is not necessarily safe.**

Japan experienced its first wave of discouragement from technical careers during the bubble years. Against the ten-million-yen salaries available in finance and property, traditional manufacturing engineers' pay looked modest. Science students at elite universities increasingly chose investment banks and securities firms. The share willing to enter manufacturing fell from 80% to 37%. This was the first blow that speculative market enthusiasm dealt to the foundations of the real economy.

After the bubble burst, manufacturing briefly recovered as overseas markets revived earlier, and it again became a major recruiter. But this prosperity was illusory. Japan's broad push for overseas expansion in the 1990s transferred large amounts of production capacity abroad. Demand for domestic research and production jobs fell sharply, while the large pool of engineers trained during the enrollment expansions of the 1970s and 1980s entered the labor market. The imbalance produced an “inflation of engineers.” Those who stayed in Japan became trapped in a cycle of low pay, extreme overtime, and fear of changing jobs. Their hourly wages were only 40% of those of their American counterparts.

More brutal still was the 80/20 division between industries. High-end manufacturing, such as cars and electronics, benefited from globalization, and its engineers shared in the gains from overseas expansion. Low- and mid-end manufacturing, including steel and textiles, had formed the foundation of Japan's postwar economy. It contracted rapidly as production moved abroad and competition from emerging economies intensified, with output falling by 40% in total. Industry choice mattered more than individual effort. Entering a high-end sector might preserve a middle-class life; entering a low- or mid-end sector could mean low pay, relentless competition, and high unemployment risk throughout a career.

## Part Four: Healthcare's Winter and Recovery

**The end of a golden age.** During the 1970s and 1980s, rapid economic growth and generous universal welfare allowed Japan to build a world-leading system of affordable healthcare. Large fiscal subsidies delivered universal coverage and low out-of-pocket costs. Pharmaceuticals were highly profitable, and doctors were widely respected. But in 1983, Hitoshi Yoshimura, director of the Health Insurance Bureau at the Ministry of Health and Welfare, publicly advanced the argument that medical expenditure would bankrupt the country. He accused the healthcare sector of greed and warned of impending fiscal collapse. His statement ignited a succession of radical reforms.

**Drug cost control proceeded through three stages:**

1. The government imposed across-the-board drug price reductions every two years. Average prices nationwide fell more than 45% over a decade. Pharmaceutical profits collapsed, research spending fell sharply, and companies missed the window for moving into biotechnology.
2. A strategy of generic substitution encouraged the production of inexpensive drugs. Destructive competition wiped out profits, and poor-quality ingredients proliferated. Major drug-safety scandals, including the Green Cross scandal, shattered public trust.
3. Fixed reimbursement by diagnosis for older patients forced hospitals to choose cheaper drugs. Service quality in public hospitals deteriorated sharply, and doctors were pushed to consider cost before effectiveness.

By the late 1990s, the number of Japanese pharmaceutical companies had halved, research spending had stalled, and foreign firms were taking market share. In 2004, the health ministry lamented that Japan's pharmaceutical industry might disappear.

## Part Five: Going Global—Building Another Japan Overseas

After the bubble burst in the 1990s, domestic demand plunged and competition at home became destructive. Japan embarked on nearly twenty years of broad overseas expansion, successfully building another Japanese economy abroad. This was one of the few sustained growth stories of the lost two decades. The expansion was not a spontaneous corporate flight. It was a carefully arranged process guided by a national strategy in three steps.

**Step One: Taking leading industries overseas together.** From 1994, Japan organized its three core industries—cars, electronics, and machinery—into alliances and systematically moved lower-end production into emerging markets. This captured local cost advantages while also catching the surge in emerging-market domestic demand. Overseas profits financed research at home. Around 2000, leading companies' market values and profits recovered to, or even exceeded, their pre-bubble levels.

**Step Two: Taking entire supply chains overseas and transforming the industrial structure.** The emphasis shifted toward the two ends of the “smile curve”: research and branding. Hundreds of smaller “hidden champions” with large shares of global niche markets developed overseas. More significantly, expansion abroad drove a deep transformation of Japan's domestic economy. Finance and property lost their dominance of market capitalization to industry, consumer goods, information technology, and healthcare. Workers gradually moved from pursuing high financial-sector salaries or secure civil-service jobs toward these fast-growing industries connected with overseas expansion. Japan was moving from an economy that caught up with others to one that others sought to catch.

**Step Three: Exporting culture.** Under the national Cool Japan strategy, anime, games, tourism, and other cultural industries expanded successfully abroad. Japan shifted from being a major source of outbound tourists to a major destination for inbound tourism. This directly created hundreds of thousands of jobs in food service, accommodation, and creative industries. People trained in humanities-related fields, including design and marketing, gained new routes upward.

**Two major opportunities for individuals emerged:**

- **Localization.** During the golden decade of manufacturing expansion abroad, from 1994 to 2004, engineers and marketing staff who followed large companies overseas became deeply embedded in local supply chains through knowledge sharing and entrepreneurship. They were often more resilient to later competition than the large companies themselves.
- **Changing fields.** The shift in Japan's domestic industrial landscape from finance toward consumption and entertainment, together with the widening of overseas expansion from manufacturing to services and culture, offered a generation new ways out of destructive competition.

The gains from overseas expansion do not belong only to the large pools of capital that move first. They belong even more to smaller companies and individuals able to grasp opportunities to localize supply chains, and to those willing to enter the new industries that overseas growth creates.

### Epilogue: Two Ways to Read *A Bowl of Plain Noodles*

The changing fortunes of the Japanese story *A Bowl of Plain Noodles* offer a fitting parable of how economic cycles reshape social attitudes.

During the bubble years of the 1980s, the story was celebrated as an expression of the national spirit: dignity in poverty, changing one's fate through education, persevering through adversity, and eventually succeeding. It resonated with a society that believed in collective striving and upward mobility.

After the bubble burst in the 1990s, a younger generation condemned the same story as a deceptive fairy tale. Reality no longer followed the straightforward narrative of effort leading to success. Once-prized careers had collapsed, educational costs were high, it was increasingly difficult for children from poor families to rise, and effort had become deeply disconnected from reward. Against the employment ice age and widening inequality, the claim that one merely had to endure long enough to succeed felt empty and almost cruel.

> The story had not changed. The age in which it was told had.

History repeatedly shows that those who join an industrial transformation early have the best chance of escaping internal zero-sum competition and seizing opportunities created by growth. Every generation has its own bowl of plain noodles. The question is whether your chopsticks are ready when it reaches the table.
