> Your amygdala is trying to protect you using methods that are millions of years old. Sometimes, that alarm goes off when it does not need to.

---

## 1. What Is the Amygdala, and Why Do We Need to Negotiate with It?

The amygdala is a small, almond-shaped structure deep inside the brain, a core part of the emotional and instinctive brain. It has one job: **detect danger and trigger a defense**. When your heart races, your palms sweat, and your breathing becomes shallow, the amygdala is taking charge of your body. Along with fear, it sets off a series of physical responses: suppressing digestion, sending more blood to your limbs, releasing adrenaline, and preparing the whole body to fight or flee.

The problem is that **this alarm system does not always judge the situation correctly**. The amygdala often sounds the alarm on the basis of incomplete or outdated information, treating a quarterly presentation as though a saber-toothed tiger were coming for you.

The good news is that the amygdala **can learn**. There are two ways to influence it:

- **Soothe it:** help it become calmer overall through healthy sleep, exercise, and eating habits.
- **Teach it:** use exposure therapy to help it learn that a particular situation is not actually dangerous.

## 2. The Two Pathways of Anxiety

### The Sensory Pathway: Information Reaches the Amygdala Before the Cortex

Apart from smell, which takes a shortcut to the amygdala, sensory information first passes through the thalamus and then splits into two routes. A fast route reaches the amygdala first; a slower route goes to the cerebral cortex for more detailed processing. This means **the amygdala can begin defending you before you are consciously aware of what has happened**.

### The Cortical Pathway: A Second Judgment After Analysis

The cerebral cortex is the slower but more precise processor. It interprets information, reasons through it, and sends its conclusions back to the amygdala. One surprising fact is that even when the cortex is damaged, as in cortical blindness, the amygdala can still mount a defense against threats the person cannot consciously see. Experiments have found that people with cortical blindness may move to avoid objects approaching them even while reporting that they see nothing. That is the amygdala working outside conscious awareness.

### The Amygdala's Language Is Physical

The amygdala speaks through the body: a faster heart rate, higher blood pressure, rapid breathing, dilated pupils, increased blood flow to the limbs, more sweating, and a sudden halt in digestion. It may also cause a dry mouth, a spike in blood sugar, hyperventilation, or nausea. When you feel these reactions, you might tell yourself: **“Wow, my amygdala is really worked up right now. You'd think I was facing a saber-toothed tiger instead of a quarterly report.”**

## 3. Soothing the Amygdala: Four Physical Techniques

The amygdala learns through **physical experience**. The book presents four established techniques for calming it:

### 1. Diaphragmatic Breathing

Sit or lie down comfortably, with one hand on your chest and the other on your abdomen. Breathe in slowly and deeply, letting your abdomen rise rather than your chest. Then breathe out slowly and let it fall. Each complete breath takes about twelve seconds—six seconds in and six seconds out—for roughly five breaths a minute. Breathing out through your mouth can make the rhythm easier to manage; pursing your lips helps slow the exhale. Continue for ten to fifteen minutes, and you may notice your heart rate gradually settling.

### 2. “Give Me Five” Visualization Breathing

Take only five complete breaths per minute, each lasting about twelve seconds. Once you have found the rhythm, add an image: **choose a color to represent your anxiety**. As you inhale, picture clean, clear air entering your lungs. As you exhale, imagine the color of your anxiety leaving through your nose or mouth like colored smoke and dissolving into the air around you. Each breath clears a little more of that color from your body. You can silently repeat “calm” or “relax” to help keep the rhythm. Continue until your body feels lighter and your mind quieter.

### 3. Relaxation Through Imagery

Choose a place in your mind where you feel completely safe. It can be real—a patch of grass from childhood, a beach in the sunshine—or entirely imagined. **Bring in all your senses.** What colors do you see? What sounds do you hear? What can you smell? What temperature do you feel on your skin? The richer the details, the easier it is for the amygdala to believe the scene.

### 4. Progressive Muscle Relaxation

This technique releases tension from the body's muscle groups one at a time through a cycle of tightening and relaxing. Start with your hands: make fists, tense them for two or three seconds, then release completely and feel gravity pulling them down. Move on to your forearms, biceps, shoulders, forehead, mouth, neck, chest and abdomen, buttocks, thighs, calves, and toes. Work through each area in turn. Keep breathing throughout: inhale as you tense and exhale as you relax.

## 4. Exposure Therapy: Teaching the Amygdala That This Is Safe

Throughout life, the amygdala keeps a record of situations associated with negative emotions: a particular person, sound, or place. It can even pick up triggers by **observing other people's negative experiences**, as when witnessing a car accident makes someone afraid to drive.

The central idea of exposure therapy is simple: **deliberately encounter the trigger in a safe setting so that the amygdala can experience the fact that nothing bad happens**. With enough repetition, the old association between the trigger and danger can be broken.

### Key Principles of Exposure

**Experience it fully, without distracting yourself.** During exposure, you need to really see, hear, and perhaps touch or smell the trigger. A racing heart and tense muscles signal that you have caught the amygdala's attention and that the exposure is engaging it. The book advises against trying to relax the body completely, since some activation is needed to build new neural pathways.

**Use relaxation techniques for support.** Diaphragmatic breathing and muscle relaxation can help keep anxiety manageable during exposure. The aim is to keep it from spiraling out of control while still allowing yourself to experience it.

**Imagined exposure can provide a bridge.** If facing the real trigger feels too difficult, begin with imagination. The amygdala responds to an imagined situation much as it does to a real one. Repeatedly imagining the same scene while anxiety gradually declines can also change its response.

**Watch your thoughts so they do not undermine you.** Negative thoughts during exposure—“I'll never be able to do this”; “What if I lose control?”—tell the amygdala that the situation is unsafe. When these thoughts appear, bring your attention back from what might happen to what is happening right now. You might silently tell yourself:

- “Take a deep breath. This won't last long.”
- “My anxiety will rise, but I can handle it.”
- “I'm teaching my amygdala that there is nothing to fear here.”
- “If I stay with this, my amygdala won't have to keep reacting this way forever.”

**Observe your body's reactions mindfully.** When the amygdala activates, take a curious interest in what is happening: “My heart is beating faster. My stomach feels a little queasy.” Accept these sensations without fighting or criticizing them. You can even speak to the amygdala: “What are you doing right now? You're tensing my muscles as if you're preparing me for battle. But this situation is safe.”

**Avoid safety behaviors.** Carrying a bottle of sedatives, having a friend accompany you, or sitting by the door so you can escape at any moment are examples of what the book calls safety behaviors. They can interfere with learning: the amygdala concludes that going there requires medication or another safeguard, rather than learning that the place itself is safe.

**Stop ritual responses.** If you have developed compulsive behaviors to manage fear, such as having to keep your hands in a particular position while driving, the book says to stop performing them during exposure. Performing the ritual reinforces the amygdala's association between the trigger and danger. Practicing without the habitual response is called **response prevention**.

**Avoid medication interfering with learning.** The book argues that benzodiazepines can suppress the amygdala's defensive response and make exposure more comfortable, while also preventing it from developing a new response pattern. When the medication wears off, the old pattern remains.

**You can complain without seeking reassurance.** Exposure is meant to show the amygdala that you can cope for yourself. If someone stays beside you repeatedly saying “You're safe,” it becomes harder to build that independent ability. But complaining is allowed. You can say out loud, “This is so hard” or “I hate this.” There is no need to pretend it is easy. Remember that no emotion is permanent. Stay with it, and it will eventually pass.

**Do not escape halfway through.** Ending exposure at the peak of the defensive response reinforces the amygdala's conclusion that fleeing was the right thing to do. The book's instruction is to remain with the trigger until anxiety falls naturally. If you do have to stop, make yourself a promise: “I'll come back and finish this.” One or two setbacks are normal.

**Learn to do it independently.** The aim is for your own amygdala to learn that you are safe when facing the situation alone, as well as when you are with a therapist. That means planning and carrying out exposure exercises independently in everyday life.

**Review the experience afterward.** After each exposure, ask yourself: How anxious was I? What methods did I use? Which helped? What could I improve next time? Then **reward yourself**. This is difficult work, and every step deserves recognition.

## 5. Anxiety Is Not the Enemy

Understanding anxiety is what allows you to get a handle on it.

Anxiety is essentially a form of **rehearsal**: imagining possible negative outcomes before an event happens. The book describes this as a distinctively human ability made possible by the evolution of the frontal lobes. It helped our ancestors anticipate the weather, store food, and build shelter. Anxiety keeps you alert to potential problems, but it completes its job only when you make a plan and take action. **Anxiety is a key; action is the door it opens.**

## 6. Sleep: Helping the Amygdala Settle Down

Rapid eye movement, or REM, sleep is the amygdala's emotional reset period. During REM sleep, the brain processes the day's emotional memories and strips away excessive reactions, helping the amygdala stay calmer the next day.

REM occurs in the later part of the sleep cycle. The cycles repeat roughly every sixty to ninety minutes, and REM periods grow longer toward morning. This is why **eight uninterrupted hours of sleep** matter so much: the latter part of the night contains the largest share of REM sleep. If you sleep for only six hours, you may lose more than two hours of rest. You may miss close to half of the night's REM sleep.

### A Full Checklist of Healthy Sleep Habits

- Go to bed and get up at fixed times, including on weekends.
- Aim for at least thirty minutes of exercise a day, at least three days a week; avoid vigorous exercise in the hour before bed.
- Stop consuming caffeine after 3 p.m.
- Avoid large meals and spicy food in the two to three hours before bed.
- Do not treat alcohol as a sleep aid: it disrupts REM sleep in the latter part of the night.
- Keep the bedroom cool, dark, and quiet, and reserve the bed for sleeping.
- Avoid all screens for at least an hour before bed.
- Dim the lights before bed and establish a regular evening routine, such as meditation, reading, or breathing exercises.
- Get plenty of natural light during the day.
- If you have been in bed for more than thirty minutes without falling asleep, get up and do something relaxing, then return when you feel sleepy.
- The book recommends avoiding sleep medication where possible and limiting its use even when prescribed.

> If anxious thoughts keep interfering with sleep, an audiobook, podcast, or guided meditation can provide a neutral voice to take the place of those looping thoughts.
